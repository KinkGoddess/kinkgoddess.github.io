A LETTER TO ANYONE CONSIDERING THE SEX WORK INDUSTRY

Before you start
making porn,
please read this.

If you’re researching how to start making porn or enter the sex work industry, I want to offer one perspective: mine. There was community, there was income, and there were consequences I did not understand until later. This is not a prediction of your future. It is the experience I wish I could have read before deciding.

This is my experience.
One perspective, not everyone’s future.

Some sex workers are happy with their work. They feel it was worth it, and they do not experience it as traumatic. I do not get to tell them they are wrong about their own lives.

I thought I would be one of those people. For a while, I believed I was. My feelings changed, and I cannot pretend otherwise. Both things can be true: someone else can value this work, and I can wish I had made a different choice.

01 / THE PRESSURE

I needed diapers.
I needed food.
I was trying to survive.

I remember being the person desperately looking for a way to make ends meet. I was trying not to get evicted. I needed diapers and food for my baby. I had no college degree, I felt pretty hopeless, and I wanted a way to work from home.

If that is where you are right now, I understand why you’re looking. I remember how urgent everything felt. I remember needing an answer that would work before the next bill came due.

At first, I wasn’t going to do anything nude or super sexual. I thought I had made a hard boundary. I thought I knew exactly what I would and wouldn’t do.

02 / THE BOUNDARIES

Then the bills
kept coming.

For me, once I was in and the money wasn’t enough and the bills kept piling up, that boundary started to feel different. Eviction felt closer. An offer to do something I normally would never have done had to be weighed against what my baby needed.

That is a vulnerable place to make a decision. It is the part I wish I had understood before I started: saying “I would never do that” is easier when you are not desperate.

I grew up sheltered, with strict rules in a religious household. Making porn felt liberating at first. I rationalized it as taking back my power, becoming a new person, deciding what to do with my own body.

What I failed to see was the future I was creating for myself, long after I would set down the camera and walk away.

What felt liberating then became my humiliation and deepest regret ten years later.

03 / THE FUTURE I COULDN’T SEE

You might not always
feel the way you do today.

I think that would have been the hardest thing for my past self to understand. I could imagine making money. I could imagine working from home. I could not really imagine being a different version of myself, years later, wishing those videos did not exist.

Working from home was nice. Making money felt good. There is no guarantee that you will make enough to live on. I eventually gained an audience through the unusual work I made and some luck with celebrity recognition and promotion. Looking back, that attention only made what I’m dealing with today harder.

Walking away from the income can be hard. Walking away from the copies is another struggle entirely. You can stop making content and close your accounts. That does not mean everyone who copied your work will delete it, or that strangers will stop uploading it.

Some people remain happy with their decision. Others feel differently over time. You might feel proud of parts of the experience and hurt by others. My regret is real, but it is not a universal outcome.

It can be hard to know how you will feel until you have lived through something, and harder still to know how you will feel years afterward. You can gather perspectives before deciding, but no one can promise which experience will be yours. You do not have to try it to earn the right to decide against it.

PLEASE THINK BEYOND RIGHT NOW

What if these vulnerable videos are still circulating in ten, twenty, or thirty years?

What if people you know find them, send them to each other, and laugh at you?

What if your family finds out? What if someone sends them to a future employer specifically to humiliate you?

What if someone you don’t like—someone who won’t leave you alone—finds your videos? What if you know he’s touching himself to your naked body, and you desperately wish he wouldn’t? You can demand removal of unauthorized uploads, but you cannot reach through his screen and make him stop.

Will it still feel worth it?

These are possibilities I wish I had taken seriously. If someone harasses or humiliates you, their behavior is their responsibility. But knowing that does not take away the hurt or put every copy back under your control.

04 / WHAT IT COST ME

I made money.
I still wish I hadn’t done it.

I used my income from sex work to get out of debt, buy a house, and go to college to finish my degree. I’m telling you that because I don’t want you to dismiss this as a story about someone who simply never made it work financially.

I did make money. I did accomplish things. And I am still telling you: for me, it wasn’t worth it.

I don’t have that house anymore. I went back into debt. I thankfully still have my degree, but I wish I had found a different way to get there. The money did not buy me the ability to erase the videos afterward.

Now I live with the fear that my past could be dragged into my present at any moment. I feel like I always have to look behind me to make sure it isn’t getting too close.

And I am angry. Angry that strangers can keep profiting from intimate videos of me while I spend my time trying to get them removed. Angry that I can leave, say no, ask for it to stop, and still have to fight the same fight again.

Sex workers are still people, but too much of the world acts as though it has not caught up to that. Some people will mock you, treat you with disgust, or want to use you and then discard you. They may reduce your whole life to “a whore who got paid.” That cruelty is not the truth about you. It is prejudice, and it is something I wish I had understood I might face while working and long after leaving.

There are reporting processes and legal avenues, and I am using them. But do not make this decision assuming that a takedown request or a lawsuit will let you undo it later. Getting unauthorized copies removed can be exhausting, and no one can promise to erase every copy.

05 / THE PEOPLE WHO STOOD BESIDE ME

There were good people.
They were my community.

I do not want my regret to erase the people who made that part of my life less lonely. The sex worker community was my family during that time. There were people who stood beside me, shared what they knew, and fought for one another.

When the outside world treats you like an exile or a societal reject, finding people who understand you matters. That was how the judgment around us could feel—not what we actually were. We were people trying to live our lives. We looked out for each other because we understood things that other people often refused to hear.

Being inside that community could make things feel less frightening. There was belonging. There were friendships. There were people who saw a whole person instead of a stereotype. Those good things were real, even though I later came to regret the work itself.

I did not feel I had enough honest perspectives on how to live life as a sex worker, including what life might be like after leaving. A lot of what I learned came from other workers: making friends, networking, asking questions, and finding people willing to teach me. Their knowledge mattered.

If you are considering this work, listen to more than one kind of story. Hear from people who are happy, people who are struggling, and people who have left. Ask about boundaries, privacy, income, support, and what happens when plans change. No single success story or painful experience can tell you everything.

There are good people in the sex work industry. That does not mean everyone arrives there under good circumstances. I was one of the people who came to it under pressure. Respecting the community means making room to talk honestly about that, too.

I want to give back to the community that was there for me.
It deserves respect.

06 / WHAT I WANT YOU TO HEAR

You deserve more
than one side of the story.

If I could go back, I would choose differently. I wish I had known more about community resources and other ways to get through the situation I was in. I wish I had understood that the emergency could pass while the recordings could remain.

But I cannot turn what I would choose for my past self into an instruction for everyone else. I cannot tell you that you will regret sex work, just as someone who loves it cannot promise that you will feel the same way they do.

I know “consider other options” can sound painfully inadequate when you need money now. I was there. I am not judging the desperation. I want you to have as much information and as much room to choose as possible, rather than feeling that urgency has already decided for you.

Please don’t assume no one will ever find out. Please don’t assume that being comfortable with it today means you will be comfortable with it forever. Please don’t assume that making enough money will make every consequence feel worth it. These warnings stay because they are possibilities I did not think through enough.

And please don’t mistake those warnings for a belief that sex workers deserve the harm they face. They do not. Being honest about stigma, exploitation, and unauthorized uploads is not accepting them as fair. People should be held responsible for mistreating you, whatever you do for work.

I am sharing this because I wish I had heard more possible futures before I started. I also want to give something back to the people who were my community. You deserve honest information without being shamed, pressured, or promised a life that nobody can guarantee.

I can’t tell you whether it will be worth it for you. I can only tell you that, for me, it wasn’t.
I knew what I was getting paid. I didn’t know what it would cost.

Leena Mae
