FOR SEX WORKERS, ADULT CONTENT CREATORS, AND FORMER PERFORMERS.

Our consent
matters.

Whether you are an adult content creator, a sex worker, someone who has left the sex work industry, or are still deciding what comes next, you deserve protection against unauthorized distribution of your intimate content. This is about consent. And I’m inviting you to help ask for change.

01 / THIS IS FOR YOU

I want a future.
I want one for you, too.

Maybe you found this page after finding another unauthorized upload. Maybe you are still creating and selling content, and someone is stealing your work. Maybe you left the sex work industry years ago, and you’re exhausted by how much of your life it still takes up. Either way, this is for you.

I don’t know your circumstances. My own fight comes from wanting to move on after leaving. But you should not have to leave—or want to leave—to have your boundaries taken seriously. You can love your work and still be harmed by someone distributing it without authorization.

I entered the industry while trying to survive. I was afraid of losing my home and struggling to afford necessities for my baby. Later, I left. I removed the accounts and videos I controlled. But leaving did not make the unauthorized copies disappear.

I want to put my energy into a life that means something to me. I want to help others. I want to wake up without feeling that my past is waiting for another opportunity to hurt me. And I should not have to prove that I have become useful, respectable, or successful before anyone cares.

Our work—past or present—should not disqualify us from compassion, empathy, or protection.

You do not have to regret your work. You do not have to explain why you started, why you stayed, or why you left. Having been paid does not make you public property.

As a country, we need to take consent seriously even when the person saying no makes sexual content for a living. Consent to create something, or permission for a particular use, is not blanket permission for every stranger to copy, upload, or sell it. Sexual content involves real people, real vulnerability, and real boundaries. Calling it porn does not make those boundaries disappear.

02 / THE BURDEN WE CARRY

We should not have to
do all of this alone.

The work of reporting can mean collecting exact links, figuring out which company controls a file, proving ownership again, and looking at material you wish you never had to see again. Sometimes the file disappears but the preview remains. Sometimes another copy appears. Sometimes you get a rejection that does not seem to understand what you are saying.

Behind every report is a person whose time, privacy, and peace matter. This is not just an administrative inconvenience. It can be humiliating, frightening, and exhausting.

I have had some removals succeed. I have also faced resistance and incomplete results. That is why I have written to elected officials and submitted reports: I want better protection, meaningful accountability, and a process ordinary people can actually use.

I cannot promise an investigation or a change in the law. But I do not want our experiences to remain invisible simply because speaking about them is painful.

03 / WHAT NEEDS TO CHANGE

Protection should be
within reach.

These are the changes I am asking for. They are proposals for a better system, not promises about what current law provides in every case.

Protection for current and former performers

Lawmakers should examine gaps that leave people without practical remedies because their intimate content is or was commercial. I want them to review the commercial-content exception in the federal intimate-image civil remedy and distinguish authorized uses from unauthorized third-party redistribution.

Consequences that make repeated exploitation a serious risk

I want meaningful consequences for people who knowingly distribute intimate content unlawfully, and stronger, proportionate penalties for repeated exploitation, especially for profit, threats, or evasion of court orders. People should have a real reason to stop before they violate someone else’s rights and boundaries. Restitution and other remedies should help the people harmed. For narrowly defined aggravated offenses, lawmakers should examine whether additional supervision or registration is justified by evidence of risk, with due process. Accountability should follow proven conduct.

Effective action against repeat abuse

Courts and providers need effective, targeted ways to stop people using uploader accounts, hosting, and payment services to continue proven unlawful conduct. Removing one link should not be the end of the response when the same abuse keeps happening.

Removal procedures that do not create more harm

We need clear reporting routes, private and proportionate verification, understandable decisions, and meaningful appeals. Reports should address unauthorized intimate previews and thumbnails as well as full files, with reasonable measures against known repeat uploads and safeguards against false complaints.

Help that does not depend on being able to afford a lawyer

We need funded legal assistance, trained victim advocates, and workable escalation when providers ignore valid complaints or operators are overseas. A right that someone cannot realistically use is not enough.

04 / WHAT WE CAN DO

If you want to join me,
start where you can.

You can write to the people who represent you. Tell them what the reporting process has cost you, where it failed, and what would have helped. Ask for a written response and for affected people to be included in policy discussions. You do not need to make your story public to contact an elected official.

Share only the details you choose. Ask how your information will be handled before providing sensitive evidence; a request for confidentiality is not a guarantee. You do not need to attach intimate images to explain why protection matters.

A STARTING POINT FOR YOUR OWN LETTER

“I am asking for practical protection against unauthorized distribution of intimate content. Please support private removal procedures, accessible legal assistance, and meaningful consequences for knowing, repeated unlawful distribution. Having created or sold intimate content should not mean losing protection against unauthorized copies. Please include affected people in discussions about reform and explain what action your office can take.”

Use what reflects your experience, change what does not, and add only details you want that office to receive.

Find your U.S. representative ↗Official House directory and contact information. Contact your U.S. senators ↗Official Senate contact directory.

If you are outside the United States, you can bring these concerns to your own elected representatives and relevant authorities. Legal options differ by country.

You can also keep a record of unauthorized URLs, dates, reports, and replies. A trusted person may be able to help without making you repeatedly view the material. Keep what you can document separate from what you suspect. Report through the appropriate channels and ask for a case reference.

If you are here to support someone else, believe that their pain matters. Do not ask to see their content or circulate it. Help with the practical work if they want that help. Support does not require access to their most vulnerable moments.

We can ask for change without putting our bodies or our private lives on display again.

05 / YOU CAN REST, TOO

This is an invitation.
Not another burden.

I am angry that we have to fight so hard for the chance to move on. But I do not want anyone reading this to feel that they must spend every day fighting to deserve a future.

You can take one step. You can ask someone to help. You can keep your story private. You can close this page and come back another day. Responsibility for unauthorized distribution belongs to the people doing it—not to you for being unable to chase every copy.

I want us to be heard. I want people making decisions about our lives to understand the harm, and to make protection attainable. I want current performers to have their work and boundaries respected. I want people who leave to have a real chance to move forward. Neither group should have to earn compassion by becoming the other.

Our consent matters. Our boundaries matter.
And we deserve a system that takes them seriously.

Leena Mae
